Bystander - How Technical Experts Are Made. By Zheng Yun. Created on 2015/6/30, last updated on 2015/10/13.

Keywords: growth, knowledge system, learning pyramid, forums, blogs, RCA, sharing sessions. Intended audience: technical professionals.

In interviews, I ask candidates: how do you build your own knowledge system day to day, and how do you make yourself better, faster, and stronger? Most engineers have never thought deeply about this question; their learning is largely piecemeal and random. In the spirit of not letting your trip here be wasted, I — who do love to play the teacher — will happily walk you through it.

## Stage 1: Seriously Build a Complete Knowledge System

More than a decade ago, when I entered the software industry, the English-language originals explaining database principles, operating systems, TCP/IP, networking, algorithms, and other fundamentals stacked up to my height; I read them conscientiously, cover to cover, and did all kinds of hands-on practice. After joining the industry, I read every classic C++ book and the original text of every protocol, laying a solid foundation, seriously.

Many engineers say that day to day they just read recommended blog posts or news on some IT portals. I call that textbook piecemeal learning — not intense enough.

At this point in the conversation, I give an example: why read full-length novels? Because a short story is like being pricked with a needle, while a long novel is like being hung up inside a sandbag and beaten from all directions with a mace — thoroughly exhilarating. To build a usable knowledge system, you have to read books. Books have structure: whether you care about it or not, whether you happen to need it at this stage or not, the book covers it. Read it from beginning to end a few times, and the needle pricks go deep.

What is a knowledge system? A few years ago, Yao Jiandong, a former Alipay architect, gave a lecture at our company on how technical people should plan their own growth, and this is how he laid it out. Technologies and skills include:

- **Computer fundamentals (theory)**: the computer model (memory/IO/clock/CPU...), algorithms
- **Specialized technical domains**: data mining, data management, intelligent recommendation, search...
- **Languages and tools**: languages and their related ecosystems, development tools, analysis tools, code management tools, HTML/CSS/JS/Ajax, common frameworks and third-party libraries
- **Debugging and testing**: debugging methods and philosophy, problem localization, bug management tools, unit testing, integration testing, performance testing, security testing, compatibility testing and methods, JS/Ajax testing and methods, service-layer testing, web-layer testing
- **Networking and systems**: the TCP/IP protocols and model, HTTP/SMTP and other protocols, Linux, network analysis tools, system analysis tools, capacity, traffic and load balancing, application deployment/standards/planning, security, monitoring and failure analysis, disks and storage, Shell, DNS and domain names, caching, reverse proxies, image servers (massive numbers of small files)
- **Requirements elicitation and analysis**: requirements document formats, requirements interviews, requirements analysis methods, requirements analysis tools, domain knowledge and experience
- **System analysis and design**: the UML language and models, analysis patterns, design patterns, domain-driven design, system analysis document formats, system design document formats, functional and non-functional requirements
- **Data and systems**: database scalability strategies, expansion strategies, backup, disaster recovery, performance, security, high availability..., data design and normalization, SQL/NoSQL, Cache, distributed files
- **Architecture design**: architecture patterns, the architectural evolution history of typical internet companies, architecture principles, common strategies, architecture design methods, understanding of non-functional aspects (extensibility, scalability, stability, consistency, performance, throughput, capacity forecasting and planning), architecture systems and related technologies
- **Process and management**: the analysis process, the development process, the review process, the testing process, the release process, the rollback process, document management, knowledge management, project management

What is above is, in fact, a foundational knowledge checklist for the profession; you can use it as a map to track down and read the relevant books.

## Stage 2: Drill into a Topic to Sharpen Your Pre-Research Skills

Whether it is company business or something you personally enjoy doing, you can abstract a general-purpose topic out of it, and then charge in the way you would write a research paper. This has to be practiced repeatedly, and practiced deliberately.

The way to work:

1. Abstract a topic — e.g., distributed locks, a distributed parallel computing engine, a framework for auto-generating FormTokens to prevent CSRF, a platform for managing and scheduling cron jobs, distributed tracing, and so on;
2. Learn from the top students — deliberately dig deep into how other companies in the industry analyze and solve problems, aggregate the various solutions, and stand on the shoulders of giants;
3. Analyze the specific application scenario, and make technology choices that balance high availability with scalability;
4. Hold a design review;
5. Run tests to prove it is solid;
6. Organize the knowledge points and hold a tech-sharing session;
7. Launch it into production, summarize the lessons learned, and hold an experience-sharing session.

One of the key points here is aggregation and sharing. In 2005, driven by the needs of a carrier-grade unified messaging service, I went and studied the SIP protocol, ran all kinds of experiments, analyzed packets, wrote a series of slide decks, and shared them publicly; for a while they were quite popular:

- SIP_to_Freshman_by_zhengyun.ppt
- SIP之穿越NAT_by_zhengyun.ppt
- SIP体系架构讲义及消息交互演示_by_zhengyun.ppt
- SIP多方会话消息之实例讲解_by_zhengyun.ppt
- SIP安全框架之认证[NTLM和Kerberos]_by_zhengyun.ppt
- SIP消息之逐项讲解_by_zhengyun.ppt

Why write it up and present it? Because of the learning pyramid theory:

- Things we have **read**, we remember **10%** of the material;
- Things we have **heard**, we remember **20%**;
- Things we have **seen**, we remember **30%**;
- Things we have both **heard and seen** (watching video/visiting exhibits/watching demonstrations/observing on site), we remember **50%**;
- Things we have **said** (participating in discussions, speaking up), we remember **70%**;
- Things we have both **said and done** (giving reports, explaining things to others, experiencing firsthand, doing it with our own hands), we remember **90%**.

This is also the management approach I described in "What Wowo R&D Did Right Over the Past Few Years": from the day people join, we deliberately train everyone to present publicly and express themselves clearly. So during the probation period, every newcomer must give one tech-sharing session and one technical review, facing challenges from all sides; sharing sessions are required in the middle and at the end of every pre-research effort; and technical lectures are organized regularly at all other times.

## Stage 3: Answer Technical Questions Like Crazy

The knowledge system gets built slowly, and the abstract topics related to the business are being explored. But this is still not enough. The world you personally come into contact with is too small; it may not pose enough of a challenge, and you may never realize how much knowledge and how many skills you lack — which works against building your ability to analyze, raise, and solve problems. So take the initiative: answer questions like crazy.

In my first years in the industry, I answered virtually every question in the vertical domains I followed (both the language domain and the business domain). I proclaimed far and wide that I would tell everything I knew and hold nothing back, published my email address and MSN (MSN carried real prestige back then), and many people would email me or add me as a friend to ask all kinds of tricky development questions — a few every day on average. I would then write up the problem-solving process in the Microsoft KB (KnowledgeBase) style and publish it on my blog.

Think about it: at work you run into a problem only once every few days on average, but this way you encounter several, or even a dozen or more, every single day. First, it keeps your brain churning; second, it exposes you to far more new knowledge.

From 2005 to 2006, work required me to learn JavaME (or by its older name, J2ME) — client development on Symbian phones in the early days. During that period I combed the posts on Chinese forums every day, striving to answer every question, especially the bugs and failures. For problems that no one had solved yet — such as real-time streaming media playback, or a two-level menu UI mimicking Opera Mini — I searched high and low, and in the end published both the approach and the source code.

At the same time, I often compiled frequently asked questions into collections and published them, for example the tricky J2ME problems I organized:

- [J2ME Q&A] An explanation of the "MontyThread -n" error reported on real devices
- [J2ME Q&A] An explanation of the "XXX has no IP address" error when WTK initializes WMAClient
- [J2ME Q&A] A response to the "untrusted domain is not configured" problem
- [J2ME] Fixing the "Cannot open socket for LIME events" error

A few months later, I became a super moderator of the Chinese J2ME forum. Through this journey, what I want to tell you is: answering other people's questions, done with the right technique — for instance, not endlessly repeating answers to newbie questions, but trying instead to crack the hard problems or the bizarre failures — will never be a waste of your time.

Why? Because you should hold this belief:

> Everything that you've learnt and all the hardships you've suffered will all come in handy at some point in your life.
>
> — Penelope Fitzgerald, *Offshore*

## Stage 4: RCA / Summaries

Now is the moment to turn your lessons learned into wealth.

What makes a good technical leader? Mention any business requirement or business scenario, and you immediately abstract it into a few modules/systems/topics and hold forth: how the industry solves it, how we analyzed and solved similar things in the past, how we should design for the current situation, what problems we might run into, what preventive designs we should put in place, blah, blah. How do you get to the point where you can do that?

First, write RCA reports. As I once said: "Since 2011, Wowo has persisted in investigating every mistake, remediating anything that goes wrong twice, and writing up every single mistake — leading by example to show every new employee that they should face mistakes squarely, disclose technical details openly, and share them with everyone. Over time, every incident and every defect that slips past testing online becomes our asset. This is our RCA (Root Cause Analysis) practice; to date we have collected and organized nearly two hundred detailed RCA reports."

The RCA report format is:

- Background knowledge (Optional)
- Symptoms
- Scope of impact
- Root cause
- Analysis process (Optional)
- Solution
- Follow-up actions: e.g., how to repair dirty production data, how to make amends for the impact on users, etc. (Optional)
- Lessons learned
- RCA type: e.g., code issue, implementation issue, configuration issue, design issue, testing issue

That way, as a qualified veteran, you have seen enough blood — and turned all of it into wealth for your life.

Second, write summaries. The saying goes: keep drawing up lists. Holding forth requires material, and all of it sticks in your memory — and surfaces at the critical moment — only if you wrote it yourself.

Well, there it is: the four stages of forging an expert, as I tell interviewees.
