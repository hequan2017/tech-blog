> A repost of Zhong Erdai's answer under the "Minimalism" topic on Zhihu: the author argues that one should first hold a clear "conviction" (knowing what one most wants to do well), and only then strip away everything that does not help—or works inefficiently—toward that goal, pouring all of one's energy into a single pursuit; only then is it possible to be the best at it. This is the core idea of minimalism. Taking doctoral study and paper publishing as its example, the post examines popular understandings one by one—"minimalism equals laziness," "a room with nothing in it," "buying only the most expensive things"—and points out that owning less is a means rather than an end; an obsessive minimalism practiced for its own sake is not true minimalism.

Here I would like to offer a few humble thoughts. This is only my own understanding, without much basis to it, and it differs from the top-ranked answers, so perhaps I am wrong.

I believe that before one becomes a minimalist, one should first have one's own **conviction**. Know exactly what it is you want to do, with a goal that is sharply defined. Only then comes the simplifying of yourself: strip away everything that gives no help—or too little help—toward reaching your goal, everything that is not efficient enough.

Human energy is finite, no matter how much people overestimate their own energy levels or those of others. Only by devoting your whole self to a single thing is it possible to do that thing as well as it can be done. If, while doing one thing, you are also minding all sorts of other things, you may very well still do them well—even do every one of them well—**but never the best.** To be the best, you must therefore simplify yourself; I believe this is the core idea of minimalism.

Since I am doing a PhD, let me use the PhD as an example: why is it that among graduates of the same research group, some publish only two or three papers in second- or third-tier journals, while others publish a whole pile of first-tier papers? It is because many of those who publish in first-tier journals are people who **do nothing all day but eat, sleep, and do research.** That is precisely why they are the most likely to become the best in their field. They do not browse Zhihu or Weibo or follow trends, but they subscribe to large numbers of research journals and follow the work of many research groups, because these things serve their conviction—say, doing research. Browsing Zhihu does not.

The above is my core understanding of minimalism.
As for how to put a conviction into practice once you have one—that is truly an art in itself, just as @Kent Zeng has written.

---

Now let me say a few things about some of the other answers.

Someone above said: eat, sleep, play Dota. True—only in that way could you possibly attain **the strongest Dota skill**. If you are a professional esports player, this is indeed your way of minimalism. The pity is that such a person usually also has to attend the occasional class at university, find a girlfriend, cram before exams just to scrape a passing grade, or satisfy every sort of expectation their family holds. Real esports players, please disregard this paragraph.

Some say: laziness. That is absolutely incorrect. Such people are not lazy in the least—in fact, they work who knows how many times harder than ordinary people.

Some say: a room with nothing in it. That, too, is incomplete. A designer's room may be piled full of drawings; a researcher's home may be piled full of papers and books, with nothing else at all. And if, as one answer suggests, minimalism means organizing all your materials into electronic form and using e-books, that is likewise a one-sided understanding. Because such people simply never spend the time and energy on these unnecessary chores. Some books are expensive and have no electronic edition at all—wouldn't scanning them be exhausting? Whether reading papers held in your hands is more efficient than paging through them on a computer also differs from person to person. Must one own these books? Yes. For a minimalist devoted to medicine, it is the absence of several bookshelves of physical books that would be truly abnormal. As for everything else, one can indeed own nothing at all.

Some say: spend your money on the most useful things—that is right; but as for spending money on the most expensive things, I would advise you to save the energy you spend upgrading your image for what is truly meaningful to you. Because simplifying yourself for the sake of simplifying is, in itself, not what I understand minimalism to be. Minimalism for minimalism's sake may indeed be an admirable attitude; but only when you truly have a conviction—when you are bent on being the best at one thing and pour all your energy into it—can you come to know genuine minimalism. Minimalist OCD is not the minimalism in my heart.

Some people apply minimalism to clothing, food, housing, and transport. There is actually nothing wrong with that, if your conviction is simply your own life. Of course, it can also be your partner, your children—these are things one can absolutely devote all of one's energy to, and weighed against doing research, building rockets, or curing ×××, such a conviction is not one bit weaker.

Of course, most answers still center on: owning fewer personal possessions, keeping nothing you do not need, and so on and so forth. These are means rather than ends; the end is to pour all your energy into your ideal. If you truly possess a strong conviction, then these practices follow naturally, as a matter of course.

Let me quote a few lines from the top-ranked answer:

> **As you take more and more things out of your life and keep less and less, you see more and more clearly what it is you truly care about.**
>
> You have the time you saved, the energy you saved, and the money you saved. As for what you want to do with them—wow, the possibilities are limitless.

Perhaps I have misunderstood, but is this answer saying that we should first live minimally, and only then find out what we truly want to do?
I find that somewhat backwards—the cart before the horse. Only by knowing from the very start what you truly care about can you possibly achieve true minimalism; with the time, energy, and money you have saved, what you want to do holds not infinite possibilities but just that single one: to fulfill your own ideal. And if the time and energy spared by a minimalist life are spent on fun and pleasure, can that still be called minimalism?

## A Digression

So, do these people count as successful?

Within their fields, they do.

But taken as a whole, not necessarily. They have sacrificed too much, because of minimalism. Abroad there are many such talents—fiercely specialized Geeks and the like; they are the Sherlock Holmeses of every walk of life. Yet most of them carry psychological problems; many have no happy family, or no mansion and sports car. All they have is the field they work in—because of minimalism; and at the same time they are the very top of that field, hard to surpass—because of minimalism.
