Good afternoon, everyone,

It is a great honor to take part in this TEDx conference, and many thanks to the TED team of Dongbei University of Finance and Economics and to Huachen Cinema for providing us with this opportunity to exchange ideas.

## Self-Introduction

I am a Dalian native and attended Dalian No. 24 High School. Because I hardly studied at all back then, I chose to go abroad after graduating from high school -- which is actually the secret many people who went overseas for their undergraduate degrees are unwilling to tell. After moving between several schools, I finally graduated from the Kelley School of Business at Indiana University in the United States, majoring in investments and financial derivatives. In school I was obsessed with stock trading and remained a slack student, so I never became one of those elite returnees who "give up a high-paying Wall Street job and resolutely come home" -- a genuine pity, since hardly any Wall Street firm was willing to have me. As it happened, the stock market and the ××× market were doing quite well in the two years before I graduated, and I made a modest amount of capital, so I decided to return to China and go into business. Today I work in the restaurant industry in Dalian. I currently own four cheesecake shops -- Buchu Erpin at Wanda Plaza, one at the Metropolis, one at Fujia Xintiandi, and Lydia Castle at Olympic Plaza -- and three huoshao shops of the Qiaodongjia Crispy Huoshao brand: at the No. 2 bus stop at Qingniwaqiao, at Parkson on Changchun Road, and one opening soon in the basement of Roosevelt.

## Introduction

This is my first time speaking in front of so many people since graduating from college, and frankly, I am very nervous. When I was younger I fantasized many times that, once I had made my name, I would be like those idols of mine and share with young friends the experience of how I rose from having nothing to the peak of life, and then tell everyone in earnest that one must live not like a blade of grass but like a tree; that only two animals can reach the top of the pyramid, the eagle and the snail; that my success can be replicated by you too; and so on. But after turning 26 I suddenly realized a serious problem: my own life might not amount to any great achievement at all. So when the TEDx DUFE team came to me and said it was fine that I was merely a snack-shop owner -- they would still be glad to offer me this opportunity to exchange ideas with so many people -- how thrilled I was. Since my company has not yet gone public, stories about grass and trees, eagles and snails, sleeping on floors and collecting aluminum cans will have to wait. Today I simply want to share a few questions that troubled me and some friends around me for over a decade, and my view of them after going through some upheavals and setbacks.

## Can Hard Work Really Make Dreams Come True?

Imagine Mr. Wang Feng sitting in a swivel chair right now, gazing at you with deep feeling and asking, "What is your dream?" And Stephen Chow's line, "If a person has no dreams, what is the difference between him and a salted fish?" is said to have inspired generations as well. Dreams are terribly important; they are nothing less than the beacon of one's life. Successful people succeed for all sorts of different reasons, yet none of them ever forgets to tell you that never forgetting their dream, no matter when, is the primary reason for their success. So much so that the most common understanding of the meaning of life for our generation is to hold fast to a dream and, in the end, realize it. But few people are willing to face one fact: most people's dreams will never -- yes, never -- come true.

You heard me right: most people's dreams will never come true.

Let me first share one of my old dreams. In college I was keen on all kinds of ××× games and was a regular at the ××× next to my school. My gambling career began with the most basic game in the ×××, the ××× bet: on the ××× there are the numbers 1 through 36 plus two zeros, and the odds are 36 to 1. The numbers 1 through 36 come in red and black, and a bet on red or black pays 1 to 1. Anyone who has been through nine years of compulsory education knows that the moment the ××× starts spinning is a purely independent random event. But the charm of ××× lies precisely here: when you are actually inside the ××× and have watched red come up 4 times in a row, almost everyone wants to push their chips onto black. My dream at the time was to decipher the mystery within. My initial strategy was very simple: after 3 odd numbers in a row, bet on even; after 3 reds in a row, bet on black. Something unbelievable happened: as long as I followed this strategy strictly, not only did I walk away in one piece on my first few trips to the ×××, I made a tidy profit each time -- to the point that I developed an illusion that the game perhaps did follow a pattern, and I could already see the peak of life waving at me from not far off. Of course, you can guess how it ended: after 18 even numbers in a row and 21 consecutive blacks, I obediently handed every coin I had won back to the ×××.

Later I learned that my foolish dream had a name: the gambler's fallacy -- I will not go into detail here. But it meant a great deal to me; I finally understood that in the face of purely random events, every pattern is meaningless.

A very few things in life are purely random events, just like ××× gambling -- the Double Color Ball lottery, for example. Yet almost everyone who has won the Double Color Ball will tell you how much energy they devoted to studying past numbers, how they researched historical patterns, how much painstaking effort they put in before finally succeeding. In truth, even in matters governed purely by chance, as long as the base of participants is large enough, low-probability events will eventually occur. The interesting thing is that nearly every beneficiary of a random event attributes a result decided entirely by luck to his own hard work. Nor is it only the participants; onlookers believe it too. Take, once more, the champion of The Voice of China.

**Everything we encounter in life falls, basically, into three categories. The first is decided purely by randomness, such as Brownian motion and ××××××; the second purely by ability, such as the CET-6 English exam or the 110-meter hurdles. The third, which we meet most often, is decided by both ability and randomness -- starting a business, investing, falling in love, or chasing a dream.**

The reason I am deeply weary of the motivational masters who always tell young people to chase their dreams at all costs is that, although most people's dreams are not pure Double Color Balls, they are most decidedly dominated by randomness. In the face of powerful randomness, however much sweat you shed, it is like crouching beside the ××× day and night hoping to crack its pattern. Speaking of the Voice of China champion -- Zhang Bichen's "you are my destiny" left me utterly speechless. But after all, at that very moment, how many girls in China sang just as well as she did, or better? If winning The Voice of China were truly one's lifelong dream, one's whole life would have to be spent in torment. I am personally fond of Huang Bo, but I would never use him as an example to spur a young man with a face like mine to chase an acting dream at all costs -- note, at all costs. Because in singing or acting, however hard you work, effort can only make you excellent; no quantifiable standard of judgment exists, and to become a star admired by all, randomness matters far, far more than ability.

I think **the most precious fortune is this: when you are young, in everything you do, you can clearly tell what share of it is randomness and accept that share with equanimity.**

So how large a role does luck play in your dream? When you sense deeply that its randomness may never smile upon you, are you still willing to press on? As for me, a dream is always worth pursuing with devotion, yet I can accept with perfect equanimity that it may simply never come true.

## If Even Dreams Cannot Come True, What Is Worth Working Hard For?

Around this time last year, I posted a Weibo.

**All these years I have kept reminding myself of one thing: never be moved by your own struggle. Most people's so-called effort is nothing but foolishness at work. Staying up all night reading until dawn, sleeping only a few hours for days on end, going months without a holiday -- if such things are worth boasting about, then anyone on a Foxconn assembly line works far harder than you. It is only human to be born with a taste for self-pity; only by staying clear-headed at every moment can you see where real value lies.**

That this passage spread like wildfire across the internet took me completely by surprise. What surprised me even more was that in the comments I saw a fair amount of abuse, and some people earnestly wrote me words of considerable depth: "When you have your own light, do not blow out other people's candles; you must not deny others simply because you dislike them." Utterly baffling, is it not? Even if you had only just heard my previous remarks about randomness, you would know I have never considered effort a trivial thing. Quite the opposite: I have always believed that before your ability reaches a certain level, you do not even qualify to face randomness. That Zhang Bichen won The Voice of China naturally involved luck, but put Yang Mi in her place and the judges would not merely have failed to turn around -- they might have bolted straight off the stage.

But now the question arises: what, exactly, counts as valuable effort? This can be explained starting from that Weibo post. Around this time last year, a friend and I were toying with the idea of a clothing business in Daqing and decided to go survey a few shopping malls. I was living in Beijing at the time. The night before, I had been out enjoying myself with friends and got home rather late; worried that I would oversleep and miss my flight, I simply spent the night propped up on the sofa. It was my first trip to Harbin; November was already bitter cold, I had not packed enough clothes, and stepping off the plane I was frozen to the point of a splitting headache. And because I had not booked in advance, I only bought my train ticket after arriving in Harbin -- only standing tickets were left. So there I was: a sleepless night, frozen dizzy, then more than 2 hours standing on a green-skinned train. The instant I arrived in Daqing, I felt I had been through something extraordinary, something that simply had to go into my memoir someday. But looking back and thinking it over carefully, these so-called "efforts" had not the remotest connection with whether I would ultimately run that clothing business well. What is more, had I simply gone to bed earlier the night before, packed warmer clothes, and booked the train ticket online ahead of time, I could have reached exactly the same goal in perfect comfort. That experience was a microcosm of so much of my twenty-odd years of life: wallowing in actions that contributed nothing to the outcome and counted only because I had suffered a little, and mistaking them for effort.

**When I finally realized that I was not the only person who had ever mistaken meaningless exertion for effort, it suddenly dawned on me that the people I had considered so hard-working in life might not be all that diligent after all -- and that if you keep acting in the right direction, surpassing them is not so difficult.**

That is because our generation's understanding of diligence and effort comes almost without exception from school. More precisely, for the first twenty-some years of life, the hardest-working people in our eyes were those who studied and drilled problems most desperately. In reality this understanding is extremely one-sided and childish, because studying and problem-drilling exist for a single blazingly clear purpose: to pass exams. Such diligent exertion is utterly pure -- more revision time and higher revision intensity will, generally speaking, directly raise exam scores; the connection between them is vivid and immediate, plain to everyone.

**But the beauty of life lies in this: many things simply cannot be understood until we have done them to a certain degree.**

It is like learning English. For a dozen years and more I fantasized about what complicated procedures, what exquisite design, what comprehensive coverage, what unimaginable effort it would take -- until one fine day, thanks to all that impeccable earlier study, I would at last speak fairly fluent English, thinking as I spoke, just as in Chinese, instead of designing the structure, tense, and vocabulary of every sentence beforehand, rehearsing it silently a few times, and then reciting it so as to look fluent. Who has not imagined it this way? Alas, it never came true, and I could see no trend of it ever coming true. For anyone who has set a goal, there is no more painful feeling than this.

But after roughly 2 years in the United States, I suddenly discovered that I could already speak fluent English without the slightest obstruction. It was not that I had adopted some new study method; it was that after arriving in Indiana there were very few Chinese people around me, and with no alternative I was forced to communicate and express myself in English. In that process I never seriously reckoned how much I improved each day, nor did I test my progress in stages; I simply kept listening and speaking -- because there was no choice, after all. Until one day, a little over 2 years in, it suddenly hit me: hey, I really can do this now. Yet I truly could not distill how I had managed it step by step; it is just that during those 2 years I had been living in English, however reluctantly.

**The most precious abilities a person can acquire are all like mastering a language: the effort you put in brings no immediate feedback -- for a long stretch you may see no return whatsoever -- until accumulation reaches a certain stage and it suddenly bursts forth with astonishing force, and even you cannot tell how it all happened. Take exercise, reading and writing, or running a business: the skill you possess once enough quantitative change has finally triggered a qualitative change is beyond the lifelong reach of most people -- not because they are too dull, but, quite the opposite, because they are too clever.**

The most basic principle that triggers human action is called the reflex: we are a species that needs instant feedback. That is why most people understand the world linearly, while in many more cases things develop through long latent oscillation followed by an explosive breakthrough. I often feel nowadays that people grasp languages, musical instruments, and painting so much more easily in childhood -- skills terribly hard to learn in adulthood -- not because children are precocious, but because a child rarely stops once a week to question how much has been gained; they simply bury their heads and practice for years on end, and only after mastering it do they discover, oh, I can already do this. Only clever adults believe stories like "understand the I Ching with one book," "the secrets of Jack Ma's success in 10 sentences," and "become a guitar master in 30 days."

In short, **in real life, effort and result are often far from instantaneous. After leaving school, once the many things we encounter are no longer so tightly bound as problem-drilling is bound to exams, most people's exertions stop at a first taste. The most precious effort is to choose a correct direction and, for the things that cannot repay you immediately, to go on giving them focus and passion, 10 years as though it were one day; the final result may not make you unbeaten and lonely at the summit, but it will be enough to make you outstanding.**

## Is There a Point in Life After Which Everything Gets Better?

All this talk so far has been about goals and attaining them. Think carefully, and our whole lives seem spent struggling from one goal to the next. In junior high, the teacher tells you that the senior high entrance exam eliminates the most people -- just fight your way through, and once you are in senior high everything will be fine. But once in senior high you find it is nothing of the sort, and the senior high teacher says, pass the college entrance exam and you are in heaven. So you pass it, and you are still empty and adrift through all those grass-like years of youth, and your parents and teachers tell you, it will be fine once you find a job. After you start working, the worries and anxieties are all still there, and your girlfriend shows you stories about Jack Ma and tells you it will be fine once your career takes off...

Have you noticed? Every stage of life brings its own new pains and worries, cycling round and round, never ceasing. You will by no means live happily ever after simply because you got into college, made something of your career, and married the goddess of your dreams. Yet each stage also has the happiness of that stage, which nothing can replace. Life is neither an Andersen fairy tale nor a Hollywood movie: from the moment of birth to the end of life, no point exists past which everything turns blissful, perfect, and carefree.

**Every stretch of years has its own reason to exist; none stands higher or lower, nobler or baser, and none should be squandered. The stupidest thing a person can do in this life, so far as I can imagine, is to stake every hope of living on some single future point while neglecting the delight life itself is owed. Even if you do one day truly attain that obsessive goal, you will find it far less wonderful than you imagined.** The joy of playing basketball on the playground with your buddies and drinking Coke when you are young cannot be replaced, later on, by sipping red wine in a golf clubhouse. Especially you men: never go on thinking about how grand it will be "when I'm rich someday." Never mind that you may well never be very rich -- and believe me, even with money it really is not such a big deal. **Life resides in each day's living. All obsessions are illusion. Getting along pleasantly with the people around you, arranging each day's affairs with care, feeling each day's state of mind with your heart -- that is the very meaning of life.** This, in fact, is the thing I most wanted to share with you today.

Thank you all.

Yu Zhou
