> **About this post**
>
> Reposted from Zhihu, a long-form essay on ways of thinking: the author argues that being "smart" is not a gift, but the ability to identify, analyze, and extract information and to solve problems — a skill that can be acquired through practice, whose essence lies in the amount of information you hold and how efficiently you use it. Smart people have more concepts in their heads, richer connections between those concepts, and faster recall. The article sums up three thinking habits of smart people: with anything new, they are not content with merely "knowing" it, but think it through deeply until it is internalized; they do not think inside the frame, but first examine the "frame" itself; and they are not content with just solving a problem — they like to use "questions" to draw out general rules and patterns from behind the problem.

I once wrote an answer on "how to become a smart person." Looking back, it has many shortcomings, but the core idea remains the same:
Being smart is, in essence, the ability to identify, analyze, and extract information, and to solve problems.
**It is a skill that can be acquired through practice.**

How does the thinking of smart people differ from that of ordinary people?
Roughly like this:

1. Faced with the same event, ordinary people see the surface; smart people see the underlying pattern.
2. Faced with the same problem, ordinary people come up with two or three solutions, while smart people can come up with a dozen or more, and quickly find the best one.
3. Faced with the same course of action, smart people consider all kinds of possibilities and know how to respond under each of them, while most ordinary people simply let things take their course and are easily left at a loss.

Do these things come down to talent, or to some unique personal disposition? No. They are, in fact, learnable skills.

Why can smart people see deeper into an event than ordinary people?
Simply because: they hold more information about it than ordinary people; they have more experience to draw on; and, on top of enough objective information and subjective impressions, they add a measure of summarizing and generalizing.

Why can smart people come up with more solutions than ordinary people?
Simply because: they have considered similar problems before, learned about these different approaches, and even compared their strengths and weaknesses. So when a problem arises, they can react quickly.

Why can smart people consider all the possibilities and how to respond to them?
Simply because: they have thought things through often enough that thinking has become a form of accumulation and a habit.
For example, I have a friend (the classic "I have a friend" story). His articles always strike you as beyond reproach — you cannot find a single flaw no matter how hard you look. Whatever objection you think of, he has already answered it in the article.
Is he naturally smarter? Not necessarily. He told me that while writing, with every sentence he puts down, he asks himself:
"Why does it have to be this way? Would this work? What about this?"
In other words, with every point he makes, even every word he chooses, he picks at his own writing from the reader's perspective, and then refines the article step by step based on what he finds. So every question you could raise has already been anticipated and answered, which is why his writing seems beyond reproach.

All of this can really be summed up in two points.
Why are smart people smart? Because:

1. **They hold a larger amount of information.**
2. **They use that information more efficiently.**

If you picture each person's mind as a "schema" woven out of countless interconnected concepts, then in a smart person's "schema," not only is there more content than average, but each concept is also linked to more other concepts, and it takes less time to retrieve any one of them.

For instance, an ordinary person might have 1,000 concepts while a smart person has 1,500, so he can always think of things others cannot.
On average, an ordinary person's concepts are each linked to 3 others, a smart person's to 5, so he covers more angles, reasons more rigorously, and leaves fewer gaps.
On average, an ordinary person takes 10 milliseconds to retrieve a concept, a smart person only 5, so within the same span of time (and the same working memory), a smart person retrieves and processes far more concepts than an ordinary person — in other words, he thinks faster and more deeply.

To achieve this, we have to come back to information volume and utilization.

Why do smart people hold more "concepts"?
Because they take in, absorb, and store more information.
Why are smart people's concepts more richly connected? Why can they retrieve them faster?
Because they have deliberately practiced "retrieval." The more times you retrieve a concept, the more things you can associate with it, and the faster you can retrieve it.

Here is an analogy. The ordinary mind is shaped like a cylinder, with concepts floating around inside it in disorder. The closer to the top, the easier they are to retrieve, while concepts that have sunk to the bottom are either forgotten or never put to use. The smart mind is shaped like an upside-down cone: for one thing, fewer concepts sink to the bottom; for another, every concept at the bottom has a corresponding "hook" at the top — like an index — so it can still be retrieved easily through certain means.

Now we finally get to the question itself.
Why does this difference exist? In everyday life, the amount of information people come into contact with is roughly the same, so why do smart people end up storing more of it?
Because between contact and storage, information has to go through a process of encoding and memorization. I call this process "internalization."

Here is an example.

There is a method called the memory palace: you encode a space you know well, record every location in it, then place the things you want to remember at those locations. The familiar spaces and routes thereby create connections between otherwise unrelated items, which makes them easier to remember.
(Though for someone with my terrible sense of direction, it probably would not help much.)

The core idea of this method is to connect new information with old, familiar information, and thereby "install" it into your "schema." The richer and stronger the connections, the harder it is to forget.

This is the first feature of how smart people think:
**With anything new, they are not content with merely "knowing" it — they think it through deeply.**

The deeper you think, the more — and the stronger — the connections to that new concept become, and the more firmly it takes root in your mind.
At the same time, thinking is itself an act of "retrieval." The more often a concept is retrieved, the higher a priority the brain assigns it, and the easier it becomes to retrieve again. Thinking about a concept raises the "priority" of both the concept itself and the concepts linked to it, which raises the utilization rate of that whole cluster at once.

This is how smart people treat new concepts, and old ones are no different.

For example, hand most people a proposal template and the requirements, and they will simply fill the content into the template.
But a smart person might ask: Why is this template set up this way? Is its structure sound? Is it really the pattern that best fits these requirements? And so on.

This is the second feature:
**Ordinary people think within the framework; smart people think about the "framework" first.**

Confronted with an opinion, ordinary people might ask: Is it correct? Is its argument strong? And so on.
Smart people might first ask: Who is making this claim? Why was it made? Does it involve the person's stance, interests, or popular assumptions? Is it inferring the cause from the effect? And so on.

The moment you set out to solve a problem, you have in effect accepted the problem's framing. And the framing matters enormously. If you are interested, see this answer: [Why do different ways of presenting information produce different effects? - Lachel's answer](http://www.zhihu.com/question/25487119/answer/30866118)

Why can some people "see the essence through the appearance"?
As noted earlier, reaching that point only takes accumulation and generalization. You have seen 100 crows, all of them black; now someone shows you another crow and asks: what color do you think it will be?
Both ordinary and smart people would say black. Fair enough.

But ordinary people might stop there. Smart people might not.
They might ask: Are all crows black? Could there be white ones?

Then, through exploration, they discover a crow called the Armstrong cyclotron jet-powered crow, and it is white.
So they keep asking: Why are all those crows black when only the Armstrong cyclotron jet-powered crow is white?
Through further exploration, they find the reasons: 1) habitat; 2) evolution; 3) diet...
Finally, they uncover one more fact: Armstrong cyclotron jet-powered crows are actually far, far more numerous than ordinary crows — 99% of the crows in the world are Armstrong cyclotron jet-powered crows. Those 100 black crows earlier were just planted by a questioner with an agenda to confuse you.

And that is the third point:
**Smart people are not content with solving problems; they like to use "questions" to distill general rules and patterns from behind the problems.**

That is about it.

After all, finding the path that suits you matters most.
